abstract: "Small island developing states in the South Pacific face extreme weather events, low energy security, and high electricity generation costs. However, the techno-economic implications of transitioning to renewable electricity across the Pacific Island Countries remain unclear. Therefore, this study simulates the existing electricity system and optimises the future renewable generation for 14 Pacific Island Countries by 2030 using the widely validated HOMER Pro to identify the least-cost transition pathways and contribute to the development of a strategic energy roadmap across the Pacific. Sensitivity analyses further evaluate the impacts of diesel price volatility, land availability constraints, storm-hardening measures, and wave energy deployment on future power systems. The results indicate that a 100% renewable electricity transition by 2030 is technically feasible and economically viable under optimistic land availability assumptions. The least-cost pathways reduce the electricity costs by 28-74% from the current range of $164/MWh-$331/MWh while retiring 810 MW of fossil-fuel capacity and installing 3.6 GW of renewable generation and 3.4 GWh of battery storage across the islands. Solar PV, wind, biomass, wave, and batteries are primary drivers for the future power system. Storm hardening measures increase electricity costs by no more than 1% while enhancing infrastructure resilience against extreme weather events, whereas land constraints significantly influence optimal capacity expansion and prevent a fully renewable power sector decarbonisation in Nauru. Despite the strong techno-economic case, successful implementation requires regulatory frameworks, pooled procurement of technologies, local capacity building, social acceptance of technologies, and private sector participation."
